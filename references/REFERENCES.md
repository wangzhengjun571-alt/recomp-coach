# Scientific references

All three are real, peer-reviewed, and verifiable by DOI. Each row states **what
it supports in this skill** and any **limitation**.

## [1] MATADOR study — basis for intermittent restriction + refeed periods
Byrne NM, Sainsbury A, King NA, Hills AP, Wood RE.
**Intermittent energy restriction improves weight loss efficiency in obese men:
the MATADOR study.** *International Journal of Obesity* (2018) 42(2):129–138.
DOI: 10.1038/ijo.2017.206

- **Supports:** the structural idea that interrupting energy restriction with
  blocks of energy balance can attenuate adaptive thermogenesis (the
  greater-than-expected drop in resting energy expenditure) and improve weight/
  fat-loss efficiency. This is the mechanistic rationale for the "insert a 7-day
  refeed at maintenance" adjustment and for the 5:2 cycling structure generally.
- **Finding:** in 47 men, intermittent ER (2-week ER blocks alternating with
  2-week energy-balance blocks) produced greater weight loss (14.1 vs 9.1 kg) and
  fat loss than continuous ER, with similar fat-free-mass loss, and a smaller
  composition-adjusted reduction in resting energy expenditure.
- **Limitation:** participants had **obesity** (~110 kg, BMI ~34). External
  validity to a lean male near 20%→13% body fat is limited; the *direction* of
  the effect is informative, the *magnitude* is not transferable.

## [2] Seimon et al. systematic review — intermittent vs continuous restriction
Seimon RV, Roekenes JA, Zibellini J, Zhu B, Gibson AA, Hills AP, Wood RE,
King NA, Byrne NM, Sainsbury A.
**Do intermittent diets provide physiological benefits over continuous diets for
weight loss? A systematic review of clinical trials.**
*Molecular and Cellular Endocrinology* (2015) 418(Pt 2):153–172.
DOI: 10.1016/j.mce.2015.09.014

- **Supports:** the claim that intermittent energy restriction yields weight loss
  broadly **equivalent** to continuous restriction, and the rationale that
  deliberate energy-balance periods may blunt adaptive responses — i.e. the
  program's cycling is not expected to be inferior to a flat daily deficit.
- **Finding:** review of 40 publications (12 with direct intermittent-vs-
  continuous comparison); intermittent regimens generally matched continuous
  restriction for weight loss.
- **Note:** this is the paper that matches the title the user supplied as
  "Reference 2." The exact published title uses "continuous **diets**" (not
  "continuous energy restriction"), and it was **not** among the project PDFs —
  it was verified against PubMed (PMID 26384657) / the publisher record.

## [3] Morton et al. meta-analysis — basis for protein-as-anchor
Morton RW, Murphy KT, McKellar SR, Schoenfeld BJ, Henselmans M, Helms E,
Aragon AA, Devries MC, Banfield L, Krieger JW, Phillips SM.
**A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein
supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength
in healthy adults.** *British Journal of Sports Medicine* (2018) 52(6):376–384.
DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608

- **Supports:** "protein is the non-negotiable anchor." Protein supplementation
  augments resistance-training gains in strength and fat-free mass in healthy
  adults, with the benefit **plateauing at a total intake of ~1.62 g/kg/day**.
- **Practical implication for this user:** 150 g/day on a ~54 kg LBM is
  ≈ 2.78 g/kg LBM (≈ 2.2 g/kg total bodyweight) — already **above** the plateau.
  Therefore "add more protein" is **not** an effective lever; protein adequacy is
  not the limiting factor. This is why the adjustment table moves carbohydrate
  and total energy, never protein.
- **Provenance:** this was the second project PDF
  (`bjsports2017097608.pdf`). The user had labelled "Reference 2" with a
  different (intermittent-diet) title; this file is in fact the Morton protein
  meta-analysis. Both papers are cited here so nothing is misattributed.
